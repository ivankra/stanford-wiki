# CS 254: Computational Complexity

The graduate complexity course, taught from Arora-Barak with Wigderson's *Mathematics and Computation* as the wider frame. Eighteen lectures cover five blocks — space, the polynomial hierarchy, randomness, non-uniformity, interactive proofs — and the arc is that each resource added to a Turing machine turns out to be worth less, or more, than it first appears: NL closes under complement, BPP lands inside P/poly, and IP swallows PSPACE. No exams: four problem sets and a group project with a peer evaluation report.

## Syllabus

Eighteen lectures in five parts, per the [course page](http://theory.stanford.edu/~liyang/teaching/complexity.html).

1. **Space complexity** (lectures 3–5): ST-connectivity and its role in space complexity; Savitch's theorem; NL = coNL by Immerman-Szelepcsényi.
2. **The polynomial hierarchy** (6–8): P, NP, coNP and NP ∩ coNP as "having a good characterization"; the hierarchy itself; P^SAT and oracle machines; what efficient computation looks like if P = NP.
3. **Randomized complexity** (9–10): randomness as a resource, P versus BPP, randomness against non-determinism.
4. **Non-uniform computation** (11–12): circuit complexity and P/poly; Adelman's theorem placing BPP in P/poly; Karp-Lipton on small circuits for NP.
5. **Interactive proofs** (13–17): the model; the #3SAT protocol; Arthur-Merlin games; Goldwasser-Sipser approximate counting; Valiant-Vazirani and Unique-SAT. Lecture 18 is an open question session.

Lectures 1 and 2 set up the grand challenges and recap CS 154.

## Prerequisites

- Automata and computability at the level of CS 154 — the second lecture is an explicit recap of it.
- Mathematical maturity: the catalog names it, and the coursework is proof-writing throughout.
