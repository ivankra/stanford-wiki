# CS 249I: The Modern Internet

An empirical networking course: instead of building protocols, students measure the deployed Internet. Three projects dominate the grade — BGP-peer a software router onto the public Internet, crawl the top sites with a headless browser, and mine honeypot, Censys and Certificate Transparency data. Guest speakers from Apple, Stanford's cyberlaw center and the measurement community fill much of the second half.

## Materials

The course doesn't record its lectures for anyone, and almost everything else it hands students is on the open site: 15 of the 18 teaching days' decks and all 3 project specs, which are 75% of the grade. Only 3 guest decks and the final go through logins.

- **Syllabus**: the [course site](https://cs249i.stanford.edu/) is one page carrying the whole 20-lecture schedule, the readings, the project deadlines and the grading split — 3 projects at 25% each and a final exam at 25%.
- **Slides**: decks for 15 teaching days, from [lecture 1](https://cs249i.stanford.edu/lectures/lecture1.pdf) on Internet identifiers to [LEO satellite networks](https://cs249i.stanford.edu/lectures/lecture-LEO.pdf), with the CDN, HTTPS-crypto and privacy-initiatives decks named for their topic rather than numbered. The post-quantum crypto, encryption-law and censorship guest lectures post their slides on Ed instead.
- **Notes**: none of the course's own.
- **Videos**: none; "Lectures are not recorded; in-person attendance expected."
- **Assignments**: [project 1](https://cs249i.stanford.edu/projects/project_1.pdf) on routing and topology, where a software router is BGP-peered onto the public Internet, [project 2](https://cs249i.stanford.edu/projects/project_2.pdf) on web and content delivery, and [project 3](https://cs249i.stanford.edu/projects/project_3.pdf) on services and security, which mines honeypot, Censys and Certificate Transparency data.
- **Solutions**: nothing published.
- **Exams**: the open-note final runs on Gradescope; no past paper is published.
- **Projects**: the 3 measurement projects are handed in rather than shown; the course keeps no showcase or archive of student work.
- **Repo**: the project specs name the tools but the course ships no starter repository.
- **Textbook**: no required book. The site points readers at Kurose and Ross and at Peterson and Davie's [Computer Networks: A Systems Approach](https://book.systemsapproach.org/), which is free online.

## Syllabus

[The schedule](https://cs249i.stanford.edu/) runs four blocks over ten weeks.

1. Routing and topology (1/6-1/22): Internet identifiers and a BGP refresher, transit and peering economics, IXPs and topology flattening, MPLS, BGP hijacking and RPKI, then Internet governance — IANA, ICANN and the IPv4 market.
2. Names and delivery (1/27-2/3): CDN mechanics, multicast and anycast; DNS, DNSSEC and failure modes; the registrar-registry-registrant ecosystem and its attacks.
3. The web (2/5-2/19): HTTP/1.x through HTTP/3 and QUIC, third-party content and GDPR, RFC 9614 privacy partitioning, eCrime and attack origins.
4. Trust and its limits (2/24-3/12): TLS 1.0-1.3 and WebPKI, post-quantum crypto and root stores, middleboxes and HTTPS interception, LEO satellite networks, U.S. encryption law, global censorship.

## Prerequisites

- Networking: Ethernet, IP and TCP, routing concepts and socket programming — [CS 144](CS%20144.md), EE 284 or equivalent. The site suggests Kurose and Ross or Peterson and Davie as a refresher.
