# CS 255: Introduction to Cryptography

Dan Boneh's course on how cryptographic primitives work and how to use them without breaking them. It builds strictly upwards — one-time pad, stream ciphers, block ciphers, MACs, hashing, authenticated encryption, then cyclic groups and everything public-key — and cashes the theory out in two projects: a password manager and an end-to-end encrypted chat client. One three-hour open-book final, no midterm. Eight years of past finals are public, which is rare for a Stanford CS course.

## Materials

Everything the coursework needs is public — the syllabus, the free book it is read against, all 6 assignments with starter code, and 27 past finals. What a reader can't have is the lectures: they are filmed to Canvas, and the free course the site offers instead covers only part of the material.

- **Syllabus**: the [syllabus page](https://cs255.stanford.edu/syllabus.html) gives all 18 lectures with readings keyed to both textbooks; the [course info page](https://cs255.stanford.edu/info.html) covers coursework, collaboration, the 72-hour extension allowance and exams. [Winter 2025](https://crypto.stanford.edu/~dabo/cs255/) keeps its whole site on the instructor's host.
- **Slides**: 6 of the 18 lectures post a deck, in PDF and mostly PPTX — [PRPs and PRFs](https://cs255.stanford.edu/lectures/lecture4-PRP-PRF.pdf), CPA security, RSA, one-time signatures, identification protocols and key exchange. The other 12 have readings only.
- **Notes**: *[A Graduate Course in Applied Cryptography](https://cryptobook.us)* by Boneh and Shoup, free, is the course's own book, and the syllabus keys most lectures to it. Two number-theory handouts, [numth1](https://cs255.stanford.edu/handouts/numth1.pdf) and [numth2](https://cs255.stanford.edu/handouts/numth2.pdf), fill in the arithmetic.
- **Videos**: filmed to Canvas — "you can access these recordings by logging into the course Canvas site". The site's public alternative is Boneh's [Online Cryptography Course](https://crypto.stanford.edu/~dabo/courses/OnlineCrypto/), a separate free course with its own videos and slides, which this one describes as covering "some of the material in class".
- **Assignments**: [hw1](https://cs255.stanford.edu/hw/hw1.html) through [hw4](https://cs255.stanford.edu/hw/hw4.pdf), plus the [password manager](https://cs255.stanford.edu/hw/proj1.pdf) and [chat client](https://cs255.stanford.edu/hw/proj2.pdf) specs. Winter 2025's set is public too.
- **Solutions**: none posted, and the info page says why in reverse: "It is an honor code violation to consult solutions to homework problems from previous CS255's."
- **Exams**: an [open directory](https://crypto.stanford.edu/~dabo/courses/cs255_finals/) of 27 finals, 1998 through 2026, with a [practice worksheet](https://crypto.stanford.edu/~dabo/courses/cs255_finals/worksheet.pdf); the course page links the 8 most recent and says the format follows previous years. There is no midterm.
- **Projects**: none; the 2 programming projects are graded coursework, and no student work is shown.
- **Repo**: starter zips for [both](https://cs255.stanford.edu/hw/proj1.zip) [projects](https://cs255.stanford.edu/hw/proj2.zip).
- **Textbook**: Boneh and Shoup's free book, above, and Katz and Lindell's *Introduction to Modern Cryptography*, optional and not free. The info page warns that "the textbooks do not cover all the material discussed in class".

## Syllabus

Eighteen lectures, per the [syllabus page](https://cs255.stanford.edu/syllabus.html).

1. **Basic symmetric-key encryption** (1–5): what a cipher is; the one-time pad and perfect secrecy; stream ciphers and semantic security; iterated Even-Mansour and AES; PRPs, PRFs and modes of operation; CPA security, nonce-based encryption, differential and linear cryptanalysis, side channels and quantum attacks.
2. **Message integrity** (6–8): MAC definitions, CBC-MAC and PMAC; Merkle-Damgard and Davies-Meyer, SHA and HMAC; authenticated encryption against active attacks.
3. **Public key cryptography** (9–12): arithmetic modulo primes and finite cyclic groups; Diffie-Hellman with the CDH and discrete-log assumptions; ElGamal and CCA security; RSA as a trapdoor permutation.
4. **Digital signatures** (13–14): signing with RSA; hash-based signatures, certificates, certificate transparency and revocation.
5. **Protocols** (15–18): password protocols and salts, S/Key and SecurID, challenge-response; authenticated key exchange and TLS session setup; post-quantum cryptography; zero-knowledge proofs.

## Prerequisites

The catalog asks only for basic probability theory, and the site calls the course self-contained. It does expect:

- Basic probability and modular arithmetic — helpful rather than required; two handouts cover the number theory.
- Experience with basic proof techniques, and some programming, for the two projects.

It is aimed at advanced undergraduates and masters students.
