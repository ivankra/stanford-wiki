# CS 366: Computational Social Choice

Elections as an algorithms problem, then everything harder than elections: allocating a city budget by vote, running a deliberation protocol at scale, predicting when opinions polarize. Metric distortion is the spine — how much welfare a voting rule loses when it only sees rankings — and the reading list tracks that literature from Copeland's factor of 5 down to 3 and below. Graded on three homeworks, a scribe duty, a take-home exam and a group survey of an open problem.

## Syllabus

[The reading list](https://web.stanford.edu/~ashishg/msande336/) is the syllabus; it runs introductory material then two deep topics.

1. Voting and game theory: plurality through single transferable vote, the Condorcet criterion, implementation concepts, Nash equilibrium and repeated games, Fisher markets, Nash and Kalai-Smorodinsky bargaining, then Gibbard-Satterthwaite and the escapes from it — median voting, approval voting.
2. Metric distortion: the original factor-5 result for Copeland, the improvements down to 3, Plurality Veto's simple proof, randomized approaches below 3, and the link between distortion and fairness.
3. Participatory budgeting and deliberation: knapsack voting, the core of the PB problem, strategy-proof mechanisms, justified representation and the method of equal shares, and sequential deliberation.
4. Opinion dynamics: deGroot, attitude polarization, Schelling segregation.
