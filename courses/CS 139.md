# CS 139: Human-Centered AI

The premise is that an AI system's behavior is only half the problem: "The course examines how mental models and user models of AI systems are formed, and how that leads to user expectations." The assignments are written and prototyped rather than trained, and the course's own AI policy is disclosure — students may use it, but must say how.

## Materials

There is no course site, no schedule and no grading policy anywhere. What is public is the coursework, in full, and it is published by Stanford's Center for Teaching and Learning rather than by the course — a year behind this offering.

- **Syllabus**: closed. All four offerings from Spring 2023 on uploaded one to Canvas, every one marked Stanford-only. The [learning goals](https://ctl.stanford.edu/aimes/aimes-library/human-centered-ai-goals) are public, but a goal list is neither a schedule nor a grading policy.
- **Slides**, **Notes**, **Videos**, **Solutions**, **Repo**: not found.
- **Assignments**: nothing for this offering. The [CTL write-up](https://ctl.stanford.edu/aimes/aimes-library/human-centered-ai-1) reproduces all 3 Autumn 2024 prompts verbatim — an essay on "what it will mean to be a learner in a world where strong AI exists"; a two-part design exercise building a party invitation first with a text-to-image model and then with plain image search, to ask "how does using prompt-based image generation change the design process of non-professional designers?"; and a Wizard-of-Oz study in which pairs simulate a human-in-the-loop system over an open phone line or text thread, run a fifteen-minute session and cut a one-minute video of what broke. The course's AI policy is quoted too: students may use it on any assignment but must disclose every use.
- **Exams**, **Projects**: none. The three assignments are the whole of the graded work.

## Syllabus

No published syllabus. The [learning goals](https://ctl.stanford.edu/aimes/aimes-library/human-centered-ai-goals) are public and are stated as open questions rather than outcomes, because "things change rapidly in AI...Even during a 10-week course" — among them which AI user experiences exist today and who they were designed for, what is at stake in data ownership, and which constituencies an AI system has to serve.

The catalog gives the arc: mental and user models of AI systems, how they shape expectations, and "a set of design guidelines for building AI systems that are trustworthy, understandable, fair, and beneficial", plus "the impact of AI systems on the economy and everyday life, and ethical issues of collecting data and running systems, including respect for persons, beneficence, fairness and justice."

The three assignments, per the CTL write-up, mark out the same ground:

1. **What will learning be like in an AI world?** — review published pieces, find one more, and write two to five pages on how AI changes a Stanford education and what would keep it "human-centered, ethical and fair", ending with a reflection on how the work was done and which AI tools helped.
2. **Creating art using AI?** — how text-prompt image generation changes the design process for non-professional designers, treating prompts as "a reflective design material aiding exploration and collaboration".
3. **A human-in-the-loop prototype** for a Wizard of Oz study.
