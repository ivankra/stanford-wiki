# CS 181: Computers, Ethics, and Public Policy

The stated aim is a reflex, not a reading list: "how to recognize (how to train a grasshopper on your shoulder to be vigilant about noticing) when a decision you are facing has a significant ethical implication". Assessment is 70% writing and project, and the class is deliberately not recorded.

## Materials

The readings and the framing are public, and the course never records, so there is nothing gated about the lectures either. What is missing is the week-by-week schedule, which the site leaves at TBD, and the short writing assignments that carry 40% of the grade.

- **Syllabus**: the [site](https://stanfordcs181.github.io/) carries the learning goals, the grading split and the FAQ, but its course calendar is marked TBD.
- **Slides**: one deck, ["The Greatest Good"](https://stanfordcs181.github.io/slides--the-greatest-good.pdf); no other session has one.
- **Notes**: about 2 dozen readings — Buolamwini and Gebru on gender shades, Corbett-Davies and Goel on fairness, the Boeing 737 Max reporting, Zittrain on automated thinking, Le Guin's "Omelas" — most of them open. 5 sit on `cs181.keithw.org`, which no longer resolves.
- **Videos**: none by policy; "the class is not recorded".
- **Assignments**: the [project guidelines](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1gbRriOP1aMHVVJgiGdFplU_A6pAG6hu3ysrBPdLxnEE/edit?usp=sharing) are a Google Doc that exports without an account. The short writing assignments are not posted.
- **Solutions**, **Exams**: none; the work is memos, oral argument and a project, and there is no final.
- **Projects**: the poster session and presentation day are internal, and no student work from this offering is published.
- **Repo**: none; the course sets no code.
- **Extra**: 11 years of student project websites, 1995-96 to 2010-11, are still served from [Eric Roberts's course page](https://cs.stanford.edu/people/eroberts/courses/cs181/) — several dozen per year, each a small site on one question (the Clipper Chip and electronic vote counting in 1995-96; Bitcoin, hacktivism and China's Great Firewall in 2010-11). Last touched in 2013, far too old to rate, but it is the only published record of what the project produces.

## Syllabus

No dated schedule. The site sets out what the course teaches: recognizing when a decision carries ethical weight, reasoning about it "consistent with the norms, culture, and experience of our discipline", and making "well-reasoned, persuasive arguments that can influence the course of events".

Coursework is "short online writing assignments (emphasizing the 400-word business memo), in-class exercises in making persuasive arguments out loud, and a quarter-long project" that "requires interacting with the world outside the Stanford campus". Grading: writing 40%, project 30%, section participation 20%, lecture participation 10%. No final exam.
