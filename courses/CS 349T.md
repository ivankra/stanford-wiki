# CS 349T: Project Lab: Video and Audio Technology for Live Theater in the Age of COVID

A course written for one autumn and one production. Zoom is bad at delivering low-latency audio to actors, good video to an audience, or any audience reaction back to the stage; four instructors from CS, EE and Theater and Performance Studies put students on building something better, to be used in a live Internet performance the following quarter. The brief is explicit that students are working to a director's deadline and must explain resource limits to people who do not have a systems background — and that the direction will shift as the production's creative needs do. Some students were invited to stay on for Winter, paid or for credit, to run the system through rehearsals.

## Syllabus

There is no week-by-week schedule; the [page](https://stagecast.stanford.edu/) instead lists what the quarter could produce, and the list is the best account of the course:

- Measure the impulse response of a Stanford auditorium, then filter the actors' audio in real time so they feel acoustically co-present.
- Extract Blender's chroma-key module, wrap it in C++, and composite two green-screened actors into one room "making believable eye contact with each other".
- Build a real-time waveform monitor and Y′CbCr color-correction controls for live video.
- Detect a held-up color-correction card on a webcam and auto-correct the exposure and colors from it.
- Measure an Internet connection's loss and jitter patterns, then design a low-latency protocol against them.
- Optimize a C++ video codec to hold 24 fps, sacrificing quality if it must.
- Drive stage lights over DMX512-over-RJ45.
- Monitor field equipment for timing and network anomalies.
- Build an HTML/JS and WebRTC front end that gives the audience "the feeling of 'being there live'", carrying laughter from audience microphones back to the actors.

The four directions the system had to improve on Zoom: audio quality and latency over unreliable connections, video quality and realistic composited scenes, audience feedback to the performers, and possibly digital puppetry.
