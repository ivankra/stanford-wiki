# CS 298: Seminar on Teaching Introductory Computer Science

An hour a week for people who are about to stand in front of a CS class — section leaders, incoming lecturers, PhD students who will TA. It works through the job in order: know the students, plan the course, write assignments worth doing, lecture, grade, watch someone else teach, then read the CS education research on whether any of it works. Graded satisfactory/no credit, with an assignment attached to each session.

## Materials

Open, and built to be reusable by anyone teaching CS elsewhere: the calendar, every deck and every assignment are public, and the decks are mirrored as PDFs on the class path in case slides.com goes away. The only thing not accounted for is recording, which no source mentions.

- **Syllabus**: the [calendar](https://web.stanford.edu/class/cs298/templates/calendar) gives each week a paragraph of its own and the chapters to read, and a [course-information handout](https://web.stanford.edu/class/cs298/static/handouts/CS298-Course-Information.pdf) carries the policies.
- **Slides**: all 8 decks, [introduction](https://slides.com/tofergregg/cs298-lecture-01-introduction) through CS education research, with [9 PDF mirrors](https://web.stanford.edu/class/cs298/static/lectures/) in an open directory.
- **Notes**: none; the decks and the set book are the material.
- **Videos**: no source mentions recording, and none turned up.
- **Assignments**: all 8, each a slide inside its lecture's deck — from "Who Motivated You to Become a Teacher?" to "Observe a Class" and "Read a Research Paper". The pre-reading has [its own page](https://web.stanford.edu/class/cs298/a0.html).
- **Solutions**, **Exams**, **Projects**, **Repo**: none. The weekly assignments are reflective write-ups and observations, graded satisfactory/no credit, with nothing to key or to ship.
- **Textbook**: *Your First Year Teaching Computer Science*.

## Syllabus

[Eight Mondays](https://web.stanford.edu/class/cs298/templates/calendar).

1. Welcome: teaching pedagogy in general, the teachers who kept you in education, and what makes CS hard to teach.
2. Know your students, your subject and your tools — audience first, then what proficiency in CS means for a teacher.
3. Planning your course and creating awesome assignments, including where to find inspiration for them.
4. Lectures.
5. Grading.
6. Online resources.
7. Observing others teach.
8. Handling feedback, and CS education research.
