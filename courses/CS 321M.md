# CS 321M: AI Measurement Science

Benchmarks saturate, evaluation methods disagree, and capability claims are hard to defend. This course treats that as a measurement problem and borrows psychometrics to fix it: fit response models to benchmark data, separate signal from annotation noise, argue validity explicitly, and design benchmarks and leaderboards that survive the incentives they create.

## Syllabus

Nineteen lectures in three arcs, per the [syllabus](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1sE6VC7BZ0QK9gF8zD2904CJy-GRebW0dg3p0oi4jGEs), each with an optional Friday tutorial.

1. **Foundations** (lecture 1): a taxonomy and history of measurement, and the shape of existing evaluation data — multiple choice, preference-based, agentic.
2. **Measurement as predictive modeling** (2-6): item-wise and pairwise response models (Rasch, 2PL, 3PL, factor models, Bradley-Terry); inference by EM, MLE and Bayesian methods; AUC and ECE; sparse, low-rank and deep methods and the cold-start problem; sample-efficient measurement and active learning; scaling laws.
3. **Reliability and validity** (7-11): signal and noise, generalizability theory, uncertainty estimation with LLM-as-a-judge as the case; diagnostic tools, random effects and decision studies; high-noise settings such as edtech annotation; validity itself, then designing against noise.
4. **Design, governance and frontier** (12-19): the AI evaluation ecosystem; evaluations in AI governance; guest lectures on red-teaming, mechanism design against gaming, and validity under distribution shift.

Assessment sums to 120%: project 40%, two assignments 40%, three quizzes 40%.
