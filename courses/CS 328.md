# CS 328: Foundations of Causal Machine Learning

Vasilis Syrgkanis's PhD course on the theory under causal ML, taught in one three-hour Friday block. It splits cleanly: first, when a causal effect is identified at all — potential outcomes, DAGs and d-separation, SWIGs, then the escape routes when confounding is unobserved (front door, IV, diff-in-diff, RDD); then how to estimate it at a parametric rate while a machine-learning model fits the nuisance, which is Neyman orthogonality and debiased ML; then learning theory for heterogeneous effects. Two of the sixteen sessions are pure probability background. No exams and no graded problem sets in the weighting — the grade is a quarter-long project plus its presentation.

## Syllabus

Sixteen sessions in three blocks, per the [calendar](https://stanford-msande328.github.io/winter26/calendar/).

1. **Causal identification** (Jan 9–16): potential outcomes and DAGs, single world intervention graphs and identification by adjustment; d-separation and the proof that it implies conditional independence; unobserved confounding beyond DAGs — front door, instrumental variables, difference-in-differences and regression discontinuity, read against Card and Krueger and the Basque Country study.
2. **Semi-parametric inference** (Jan 23 – Feb 13): a background lecture on concentration and the CLT; Neyman orthogonality and debiased machine learning, with proofs of asymptotic linearity with and without sample splitting, automatic debiased ML, and the Lasso rate; dynamic treatment regimes; proxies and instruments.
3. **Statistical learning theory** (Feb 20 – Mar 6): Rademacher complexity and concentration inequalities; learning theory proper; learning theory for heterogeneous treatment effects; U-statistics, then non-parametric confidence intervals from random forests. Student presentations close the term.

## Prerequisites

- A good theoretical understanding of probability and statistics — the course proves rates and asymptotic normality, and gives two sessions over to the background it needs.
- Some familiarity with causal inference is "optimal" but not required.

It is described as an advanced PhD course.
