# CS 64: Computation for Puzzles and Games

1 unit, run once, and unusually candid about what it is for: "It is intended to be laid-back and fun, and I hope to get you (even more) excited about various aspects of CS theory and AI." Wednesdays are lectures; Fridays are "purely for fun -- I will bring various problems / puzzles to solve."

## Materials

The whole course is one public page and it withholds nothing: 8 decks for the 9 lectures, a reading list under every session, the instructor's own solver code and his own example project. There is no coursework to gate.

- **Syllabus**: the [page](https://web.stanford.edu/class/cs64/) gives the format, the 2 routes to a satisfactory grade, and a dated tentative schedule of all 9 lectures.
- **Slides**: [8 decks](https://web.stanford.edu/class/cs64/0928.pdf), named by date. The 9th lecture, "Unstructured Q&A with a very special guest (Don Knuth)", is marked "no slides".
- **Notes**: each schedule entry carries 4 to 8 outside links — papers, playable engines, articles and videos.
- **Videos**: no recording of a session is mentioned. The YouTube links on the schedule are reading, not recordings of this class.
- **Assignments**: none by design; credit comes from attending 6 of the 9 lectures or doing an optional project.
- **Solutions**: none; the Friday puzzles are for fun and nothing is handed in. **Exams**: none.
- **Projects**: the brief is a sentence — "The project should involve writing at least some code… a 1-page summary of what you found would be fine" — and the instructor posts [his own mini-project](https://web.stanford.edu/class/cs64/tullis399project.pdf), on a 1-2-3-4 frequency-square fillomino puzzle, as the worked example. No student project is published.
- **Repo**: [`lightsout.py`](https://web.stanford.edu/class/cs64/lightsout.py), the instructor's Lights Out solver, linked from the lecture on that game.

## Syllabus

From the [tentative schedule](https://web.stanford.edu/class/cs64/), each session pairing a deck with a reading list. Two examples:

- **28 September**: "How to win (and lose) at Scrabble", plus "Ghost" and tries. Links: the Quackle Scrabble AI, an IJCAI paper on inferring an opponent's tiles, an article arguing Scrabble AIs "still have a long way to go", and 2 cases of teams incentivized to lose — Olympic badminton disqualifications and Barbados 4-2 Grenada.
- **5 October**: "The obligatory chess day! Cheating at chess, since it's on everyone's mind right now. The Stockfish and Leela AIs."

Grading is by attendance at 6 of 9 lectures, or "a small investigation / project in which you explore a topic of interest to you that pertains to puzzles/games… The main goal is to encourage you to explore something that you're passionate about."
