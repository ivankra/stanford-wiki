# CS 134: Introduction to AI Governance

Taught by people who write the rules rather than only study them: the lead instructor is a vice chair of the EU's first General-Purpose AI Code of Practice. Two units, one lecture a week, capped at 30, and open to any background — the assessment lets students choose weekly short papers or a single long one that may be technical or written.

## Materials

Unusually complete for a 2-unit course: the syllabus, a deck for every one of the 11 sessions, the whole reading list and the assignment brief in full, with nothing behind a login. The site welcomes auditors in as many words, and the only thing it leaves out is recordings, which it warns in advance it may not make.

- **Syllabus**: the [single-page site](https://web.stanford.edu/class/sts14/) — 11 dated sessions with paragraph-long summaries, logistics, attendance and late-day policies, the grading breakdown with its letter-grade table, and the auditing policy.
- **Slides**: [11 decks](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1GBelQn_x-YuYmmtculBW27xo8lFGPcGK/view), one per session, all Google Drive files between 5 and 45 MB, and all 11 download without an account.
- **Notes**: about 50 readings listed week by week, required and optional, mostly arXiv, plus 3 guides on how to read a research paper.
- **Videos**: "Course slides and lecture recordings (if available) will be linked below, though we do not guarantee that all lectures will be recorded, especially with guest speakers." Every session links its slides and none links a recording.
- **Assignments**: the brief is written out — the choice between a 2-page paper for each of lectures 2 to 9 and a single final long paper, what each has to demonstrate, and the rule that whichever score is higher counts for the 85%.
- **Solutions**, **Exams**, **Repo**: none; the work is written governance papers.
- **Projects**: a final long paper may run technical experiments, and no student work is published.

## Syllabus

11 sessions from the [curriculum](https://web.stanford.edu/class/sts14/):

1. **Foundations** — a brief introduction to AI/ML; what AI governance is and why we need it.
2. **Data** — balancing the need for data with transparency and copyright.
3. **Evaluation** — what makes good evaluations, and why reproducibility, validity and statistical significance are the live problems. The summary argues for "safe harbor" provisions "to enable independent, good-faith assessments free from corporate or legal constraints".
4. **Case study** — DeepSeek.
5. **Attacks** — jailbreaks, adversarial attacks and red teaming.
6. **Release** — open versus closed source models.
7. **Jurisdictions** — how the US, the EU and China govern AI.
8. **International** — existing and proposed global frameworks.
9. **Values** — implicit values in AI systems, across global perspectives.
10. **Outlook** — the future of AI and AI governance.

Grading is 85% the final assignment, 10% attendance, 5% feedback.

## Prerequisites

None official. The site recommends "some knowledge about machine learning and statistics", and enrollment is first-come, first-served.
