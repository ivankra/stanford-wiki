# CS 343D: Domain-Specific Programming Models and Compilers

Fred Kjolstad's paper course on why general-purpose compilers give up where domain-specific ones do not, taught from the line of work he helped build: Halide for dense scheduling, taco for sparse tensor algebra, DISTAL for distribution, the Sparse Abstract Machine for hardware. The organizing idea is the separation of algorithm, schedule and data representation, and the sparse iteration model gets three consecutive lectures. Half the term is project: a pitch, two discussion sessions and two demo days, alongside a 3-5 page essay whose brief is unusual — formulate a programming problem that matters to you and explicitly do **not** propose a solution.

## Syllabus

Twenty sessions over ten weeks, per the [schedule](https://cs343d.github.io/).

1. **Weeks 1–2, what a DSL is**: little languages and the case for domain-specific compilers; building embedded DSLs, with Terra; collection-oriented languages back to SETL.
2. **Weeks 3–4, dense and sparse**: Halide and the decoupling of algorithm from schedule; sparse programming systems from Codd onward; two lectures deriving the sparse iteration model from Kjolstad's thesis.
3. **Weeks 5–6, compilation**: compilers for ML, read against JAX's tracing paper and PyTorch 2; fast compilers — Firefox's baseline WebAssembly compiler and copy-and-patch.
4. **Weeks 7–9, accelerators and generation**: portable compilation with Triton and DISTAL; automated GPU kernel generation with KernelBench and AlphaEvolve; domain-specific compilers for hardware; scheduling divergent recursive workloads; Mirage for superoptimizing tensor programs.
5. **Throughout**: a project pitch in week 5, two discussion sessions, and two demo days in week 10.

## Prerequisites

- Compilers: [CS 143](CS%20143.md) or equivalent. The assignments are LLVM code generation, so the IR and its APIs are assumed.
- C++ in practice, since both starter repositories are LLVM-based.
