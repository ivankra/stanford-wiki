# CS 357S: Formal Methods for Computer Systems

Caroline Trippel's seminar sits on a specific claim from its own description: hardware projects already spend 80% of their time on verification, 70% of them ship critical bugs anyway, and richer ISAs will make both numbers worse. Four introductory lectures cover the tooling — SAT, SMT, symbolic execution, synthesis, interactive theorem proving — and then the course turns into paper discussions across memory consistency (three sessions), cache coherence, security (three), functional correctness and specialized hardware, with guests on theorem proving and Intel-style datapath verification. Half the grade is a quarter-long research project, which must ship a reproducible artifact.

## Syllabus

Twenty sessions over ten weeks, per the [schedule](https://web.stanford.edu/class/cs357s/).

1. **Weeks 1–2, the toolbox** (1–4): propositional logic, SAT and resolution; DPLL and bounded model checking, read against *Symbolic Model Checking without BDDs*; first-order logic, SMT, DPLL(T) and finite model finding; symbolic execution and program synthesis.
2. **Weeks 3–4, memory**: interactive theorem proving as a guest lecture; three sessions on memory consistency models.
3. **Weeks 5–6, coherence and security**: cache coherence; three sessions on security, opening with cache attacks.
4. **Weeks 7–9, correctness and hardware**: two sessions on functional correctness; performance; two on specialized hardware and reliability; formal datapath verification via symbolic simulation.
5. **Week 10**: project presentations, with the report and artifact due in finals week.

From session 5 the readings are student-led: each student signs up to summarize, present and discuss a paper.

## Prerequisites

Either side of the intersection is enough:

- Computer architecture: [CS 282](CS%20282.md) (EE 282), or [CS 180](CS%20180.md) (EE 180) with instructor consent.
- Formal methods: [CS 257](CS%20257.md), the same instructor's automated reasoning course, or equivalent.

Aimed at PhD, masters and advanced undergraduate students.
