# CS 204: Computational Law

Run out of CodeX, Stanford's legal informatics centre, and taught as eight guest lectures — a law librarian on legal search, Dan Katz on quantitative prediction, Harry Surden on legal NLP, Margaret Hagan on legal design. Teams of three to four spend the quarter building a legal-tech prototype with a business plan, pitched in week 9.

## Materials

All four pages of the site are public; only the weekly exercises stay inside Ed.

- **Syllabus**: the [home page](http://complaw.stanford.edu/stanford/index.php) lists the eight dated talks with speakers, and [lessons](http://complaw.stanford.edu/stanford/lessons.php) expands each week into four or five specific topics — Boolean versus semantic search over Westlaw and Lexis, named-entity recognition on legal documents, prompt engineering and RAG for legal work.
- **Assignments**: [assessment](http://complaw.stanford.edu/stanford/assessment.php) gives the 30/70 split and the project rubric (prototype 35%, business plan 15%, presentation 20%), and [timeline](http://complaw.stanford.edu/stanford/timeline.php) the week-by-week deliverables.
- **Exams**: none.

## Syllabus

Nine weeks ([lessons](http://complaw.stanford.edu/stanford/lessons.php)): introduction to legal technology — its history, the startup landscape, adoption and regulatory barriers; legal data and search, from print research to Boolean and semantic queries over legal databases and citation systems; statistical analysis in law, including predictive analytics for case outcomes and its limits; NLP for legal text — classification, clustering, entity recognition; applied LLMs, prompt engineering and RAG for augmented legal workflows and agents; computational law proper, the representation of regulations in computable form; designing for legal; productizing legal informatics; then final presentations.

## Prerequisites

- None stated; the course takes law and CS students together and the project expects at least one team member who can build.
