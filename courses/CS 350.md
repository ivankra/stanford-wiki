# CS 350: Secure Compilation

A compiler that preserves the meaning of a program need not preserve its security: an attacker at the target level has powers the source language ruled out. This course, at the join of programming languages and security, asks what criterion a compiler should meet instead, what architecture support makes the criterion achievable, and how to prove the result — mostly by backtranslation, constructing a source-level attacker from a target-level one.

## Syllabus

The [outline](https://theory.stanford.edu/~mp/mp/CS350-2019.html) is seven notions:

1. **Motivation** for secure compilation.
2. **Security through equivalences and traces**: program equivalences for security, expressing security properties and hyperproperties with traces, the hypersafety/safety/liveness diagram, with confidentiality, integrity and noninterference as examples.
3. **Full abstraction** (FAC): the definition, why it means security, attacks and threat model, violations, common mistakes, and how to prove it — what a backtranslation is, context versus trace backtranslation, and when to use each.
4. **Trace-preserving compilation**: definition, justification, attacks, and the analogy with full abstraction.
5. **Proving full abstraction** in detail, from a recap of operational semantics through source and target languages to compiler correctness and backtranslation correctness.
6. **Robustness**: robust property preservation, property-ful and property-free criteria and their equivalence, backtranslation classes, and robustly safe compilation.
7. **Proving robustly safe compilation**.

The catalog states the same in five lines: threat models, formal criteria, their security relevance, the security architectures that make them achievable, and proof techniques focused on backtranslation.
