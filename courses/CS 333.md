# CS 333: Algorithms for Interactive Robotics

Sadigh on the algorithmic problem of acting among people rather than around them: bots on social platforms, assistants brokering transactions, vehicles sharing a road with human drivers, robots in homes. The common thread is that the other agent is a person whose behavior has to be modeled rather than treated as noise.

## Materials

Nothing is served under the course code; the site is on the instructor's lab host. What it publishes is the reading list, which for a paper-reading course is most of the content. The lecture notes are on Canvas.

- **Syllabus**: the [Winter 2022 site](https://iliad.stanford.edu/cs333/2022/) — a dated ten-week timeline, each session with its papers and deadlines, plus the format, recommended prerequisites and learning objectives.
- **Notes**: the readings themselves, around thirty papers each linked to a public copy — Ross and Bagnell on reductions for imitation learning, maximum-entropy IRL, maximum-margin planning, conservative Q-learning, "Should I Run Offline Reinforcement Learning or Behavioral Cloning?".
- **Slides**: closed. "All lecture notes will be posted under the Files section on canvas."
- **Assignments**: the course policies and a [sample long review](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1_3HggWv6Kjf7uQBTRLwkULgoybcpNmnNuaTAQ-zinB8/edit) are public Google Docs — the sample is a full worked review of one of Sadigh's own papers, which is the clearest statement of what the assignment wants. The `handouts/` PDFs the timeline links (first-day questions, first lecture notes, a LaTeX template) all 404.
- **Exams**: none. **Projects**: each student runs one and presents it; nothing is published.
- **Extra**: a second public Google Doc, linked as course guidance, is a practical guide to running studies on Amazon Mechanical Turk — how to price a HIT, what qualifications to require, when it is fair to reject a worker.

## Syllabus

The [timeline](https://iliad.stanford.edu/cs333/2022/) is a reading course with lectures around it:

1. **Weeks 1–2**: learning from demonstrations, over two lectures; then learning from *suboptimal* demonstrations and offline reinforcement learning, read as a pro/con debate between two papers.
2. **Middle weeks**: coordination in repeated interactions, influencing interactions, intent inference, shared autonomy — the catalog's list — with guest lectures.
3. **Throughout**: two presenter slots per student (one pro, one con) and one respondent slot, a long review due at noon on the day its paper is presented, and a research project carried from proposal through milestones to a final report.
