# CS 352B: Blockchain Governance

A CS number taught in a law-school classroom and graded on papers. The question is how a DAO makes decisions once the code is deployed: who votes, who delegates, what a constitution means without a state to enforce it, and what happens when governance is attacked. MakerDAO opens the course as a worked case; Uniswap's delegation closes it. Guests from the web3 space take a third of the sessions.

## Materials

The structure is public in unusual detail — eighteen classes each with their own summary — but every reading and prompt lives on Canvas.

- **Syllabus**: the [syllabus page](https://cs352b.stanford.edu/syllabus.html) gives three parts, eighteen dated classes and a paragraph on each, plus the prerequisite background readings (the Bitcoin whitepaper and named sections of the Ethereum developer docs) for anyone without [CS 251](CS%20251.md) or LAW 1043. The [policy page](https://cs352b.stanford.edu/info.html) gives grading — written assignments 20%, attendance 20%, midterm paper 20%, final paper 40% — and the paper lengths.

- **Exams**: none, by design — "Elements used in grading: Homework and papers. There are no examinations."

## Syllabus

[Eighteen classes](https://cs352b.stanford.edu/syllabus.html) in three parts.

1. The anatomy of DAOs (Apr 2-23): MakerDAO as a case study, then a taxonomy — protocol, investment, social, public-funding, industrial (DePIN) and network-state DAOs — followed by smart contracts and blockchain voting, two sessions on governance methods, and auctions for contestable control.
2. Legal and regulatory implications (Apr 26-May 7): regulatory frameworks, legal considerations of DAOs, oracle and dispute-resolution design, legal design worldwide, private international law and developer liability.
3. DAOs in practice (May 10-31): zero-knowledge proofs and privacy, institutional investors in governance, governance attacks and exploits, restaking, delegation in the Uniswap protocol, research directions, and a closing AMA.

## Prerequisites

Nothing required. The catalog says "the course presumes some basic familiarity with blockchain and cryptocurrencies, but deep technical facility is not required" — [CS 251](CS%20251.md) or LAW 1043 is "more than enough", and the syllabus sets background readings for anyone without them.
