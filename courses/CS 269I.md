# CS 269I: Incentives in Computer Science

Aviad Rubinstein's course on designing systems whose users have their own reasons for what they do. It pairs each economic idea with a system that already embodies it: BGP routing for network protocol design, Bitcoin for cryptocurrency incentives, eBay's feedback for reputation, Facebook's ad auction for mechanism design, Mechanical Turk for contests, Uber and Lyft surge pricing for dynamic pricing. It is aimed at computer scientists rather than economists — the stated prerequisites are [CS 106B](CS%20106B.md) and [CS 161](CS%20161.md) — and runs three lectures a week plus a large discussion section.

## Syllabus

No schedule is published. The catalog gives the scope and the case studies:

> Many 21st-century computer science applications require the design of software or systems that interact with multiple self-interested participants. This course will provide students with the vocabulary and modeling tools to reason about such design problems. Emphasis will be on understanding basic economic and game theoretic concepts that are relevant across many application domains, and on case studies that demonstrate how to apply these concepts to real-world design problems.

Topics: auction and contest design, equilibrium analysis, cryptocurrencies, design of networks and network protocols, reputation systems, social choice, and social network analysis. Case studies: BGP routing, Bitcoin, eBay's reputation system, Facebook's advertising mechanism, Mechanical Turk, and dynamic pricing in Uber/Lyft.

## Prerequisites

- Programming: [CS 106B](CS%20106B.md) or CS 106X.
- Algorithms: [CS 161](CS%20161.md) — the course reasons about equilibria computationally, not just economically.
- No economics background is assumed; the course supplies the game theory.

Permission of the instructor substitutes for both.
