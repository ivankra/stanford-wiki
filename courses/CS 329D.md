# CS 329D: Machine Learning Under Distributional Shifts

The course starts from a gap in the benchmark story: accuracy on image classification, speech recognition and question answering has climbed year on year, while the same models "fail consistently on atypical examples and domains not contained within the training data". It surveys where shift arises and then gives it theoretical treatment — characterizations and learning bounds — rather than treating robustness as an engineering patch. The instructor's own research is in distributionally robust optimization, which is the technical center of gravity.

## Materials

The whole course is public at [thashim.github.io/cs329D](https://thashim.github.io/cs329D/) — note the capital D — and reads as a reading course with a project attached.

## Syllabus

From the course's own schedule, in five blocks.

1. **Taxonomy** (Apr 3–10): what a covariate shift is, how shifts arise, and handling them under distributional overlap — read against shortcut learning.
2. **Domain adaptation theory** (Apr 12–17): when shift is *provably* learnable, generalization bounds under shift, and adversarial approaches to neural adaptation.
3. **Neural and representation-based methods** (Apr 19 – May 1): guarantees from representational indistinguishability, self-training and self-supervision, then an empirical reckoning — "how do different robustness interventions fare in practice? Can (data augmentation / unlabeled data / bigger models) help?"
4. **Robustness and domain generalization** (May 3–15): shifts as causal interventions, invariance as a tool for causal inference, then minimax methods — robustness as "a minimax game between nature and the model", including "pitfalls and pessimism from worst-case bounds".
5. **Adversarial robustness** (May 17–31): adversarial examples, heuristic defenses and why they fail, provable defenses; then data poisoning through robust statistics and high-dimensional mean estimation.

Projects are reported on 5 June and presented on 7 June. The catalog's summary is that the course introduces "the variety of areas in which distributional shifts appear, as well as provide theoretical characterization and learning bounds for distribution shifts".
