# CS 329D: Machine Learning Under Distributional Shifts

The course starts from a gap in the benchmark story: accuracy on image classification, speech recognition and question answering has climbed year on year, while the same models "fail consistently on atypical examples and domains not contained within the training data". It surveys where shift arises and then gives it theoretical treatment — characterizations and learning bounds — rather than treating robustness as an engineering patch. The instructor's own research is in distributionally robust optimization, which is the technical center of gravity.

## Materials

The reading list, the discussion format and the project brief are all public at [thashim.github.io/cs329D](https://thashim.github.io/cs329D/) (note the capital D), and every assigned paper opens. The 10 lectures are the gap: no deck, no notes, and their recordings go to Canvas.

- **Syllabus**: the [schedule](https://thashim.github.io/cs329D/schedule/) dates all 10 lectures and 9 discussions, gives each lecture 2 or 3 learning points, and doubles as the reading list: 18 papers, every one linked to an arXiv, ACL or Semantic Scholar copy, from Shimodaira's covariate-shift reweighting and Ben-David's *A Theory of Learning from Different Domains* through Invariant Risk Minimization, Group DRO, randomized smoothing and *Poisoning Web-Scale Training Datasets is Practical*. The [course policies](https://thashim.github.io/cs329D/about/) page carries the 50/50 split, the 3 late days and the rule that "summaries cannot be submitted after the lecture".
- **Slides**, **Notes**: nothing. The schedule's bullet points are all the site says about what a lecture contains.
- **Videos**: "We will make our best effort to record and post lectures and discussions on Canvas in a timely fashion." No public set exists, in this offering or either earlier one.
- **Assignments**: the [discussion format](https://thashim.github.io/cs329D/discussions/) is specified rather than handed out, down to the 40-minute structure: 2 students write a pros-and-cons review of each paper and present the critique, others are assigned as question-askers and must each ask at least one question. The [project brief](https://thashim.github.io/cs329D/project/) asks for one method or setting from the course applied to a shift task of the student's choice.
- **Solutions**, **Exams**, **Repo**: none. The work is reviews and a self-chosen project, with no exam and no starter code.
- **Projects**: no student report is published.

Autumn 2021's [schedule](https://web.archive.org/web/20220120020449/https://thashim.github.io/cs329D/schedule/) survives as an archive snapshot, covering the same areas in the same order.

## Syllabus

From the course's own schedule, in five blocks.

1. **Taxonomy** (Apr 3–10): what a covariate shift is, how shifts arise, and handling them under distributional overlap — read against shortcut learning.
2. **Domain adaptation theory** (Apr 12–17): when shift is *provably* learnable, generalization bounds under shift, and adversarial approaches to neural adaptation.
3. **Neural and representation-based methods** (Apr 19 – May 1): guarantees from representational indistinguishability, self-training and self-supervision, then an empirical reckoning — "how do different robustness interventions fare in practice? Can (data augmentation / unlabeled data / bigger models) help?"
4. **Robustness and domain generalization** (May 3–15): shifts as causal interventions, invariance as a tool for causal inference, then minimax methods — robustness as "a minimax game between nature and the model", including "pitfalls and pessimism from worst-case bounds".
5. **Adversarial robustness** (May 17–31): adversarial examples, heuristic defenses and why they fail, provable defenses; then data poisoning through robust statistics and high-dimensional mean estimation.

Projects are reported on 5 June and presented on 7 June. The catalog's summary is that the course introduces "the variety of areas in which distributional shifts appear, as well as provide theoretical characterization and learning bounds for distribution shifts".
