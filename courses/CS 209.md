# CS 209: Law, Order, & Algorithms

Taught jointly by a computational social scientist and a law professor, and capped at twenty by application. The subject is what happens when a judge's sentencing, a regulator's enforcement or an employer's shortlist is produced by a model: which legal principles apply, which statistical definitions of equity conflict with each other, and how anti-discrimination law might have to change. Concepts are worked through in guided in-class coding exercises; the grade is response papers, participation and a final project.

## Materials

The course site is gone and its syllabus went to Canvas, but the coding half survives as a public repository from the **Spring 2020** offering: every week's exercises, with answers and the data behind them.

- **Syllabus**: closed. There is no site under any of the four cross-listed codes, the application URL the catalog prints is a 404, and the one syllabus ever uploaded — this offering's, under LAW 2023 — is Stanford-only. The Spring 2020 term has no upload at all.
- **Slides**, **Notes**, **Videos**: not found for either offering.
- **Assignments**: 8 weekly [R notebooks](https://github.com/stanford-policylab/law-order-algo) of guided exercises — an R and dplyr introduction, discrimination tests, law-school admissions, included-variable bias over two weeks, bias in food-inspection scores, and fairness over two more.
- **Solutions**: an answers notebook beside every exercise notebook, which is rare and makes the set usable alone.
- **Exams**: none. **Projects**: teams write a research paper and present it; nothing is published.
- **Repo**: the same repository is an R project, with a `get_data.sh` and cleaning scripts for COMPAS recidivism scores, North Carolina data and stop-and-frisk records.

## Syllabus

The repository's week numbering gives the coding spine: R and dplyr, then discrimination tests, law-school admissions, included-variable bias (twice), bias in food-inspection scores, and fairness (twice). The catalog gives the rest:

1. How predictive algorithms displace human decisions: risk scores in sentencing, predicted violations in enforcement, demographic targeting in advertising, machine-learned models in hiring and evaluation.
2. The concern that algorithmic decisions replicate or exacerbate human bias.
3. Legal and ethical principles for assessing the equity of algorithms.
4. Statistical techniques for designing fairer systems.
5. How anti-discrimination law and algorithm design may each need to evolve to account for machine bias.

Guided in-class coding exercises run alongside the readings.
