# CS 273D: Generalization and Causality in Biohealth

A literature course on the gap between benchmark performance and mechanistic validity in biomedicine: why models that look superhuman break at a new hospital or in a new cell type, and which causal and mechanistic commitments make a generalization claim defensible. Two failure modes are kept apart throughout — generalization failure under distribution shift, and identification failure, where a model learns associations rather than mechanism. Graded on participation (20%), a paper presentation (30%) and a project (50%).

## Syllabus

From the [calendar](https://ebfox.github.io/stats354/calendar/), five modules over eleven weeks, with roughly half of each week given to student paper presentations and four guest lectures.

1. **The generalization gap**: shift taxonomies read causally, selection and collider bias, feedback and performativity; a causal-inference primer (DAGs, do-operator, backdoor criterion, ATE/ATT/CATE); virtual cell models, hope vs hype.
2. **Mechanistic and hybrid models**: an inductive-bias taxonomy with biomedical case studies; neural ODEs, universal differential equations and physics-informed networks applied to glucose dynamics, pharmacokinetics and wearables.
3. **Causal representations and interventions**: invariance across environments, identifiability of latent causal variables, disentanglement; perturbation screens as the biological analogue of RCTs; flow matching and foundation models for perturbation prediction, and how they are benchmarked.
4. **Decisions and transport**: policy learning from observational data, propensity weighting and doubly robust off-policy evaluation; Pearl's transportability framework, the cell line → organoid → animal → patient evidence ladder, synthetic control arms and real-world evidence.
5. **Frontiers**: whether LLM agents can reason causally about biology, then a synthesis into a checklist for mechanistic generalization claims.
