# CS 228: Probabilistic Graphical Models: Principles and Techniques

The course behind the Koller and Friedman book, taught in its own order: represent a domain as a Bayesian or Markov network, then answer queries about it exactly and approximately, then learn the parameters and finally the graph itself. Five homeworks pairing proofs with code carry 70% of the grade and a final exam the rest — no project. Its best-known artefact is the open course-notes site, which students are invited to improve by pull request for extra credit.

## Materials

The schedule and a complete set of course notes are public; the coursework is not.

- **Syllabus**: the [course site](https://ermongroup.github.io/cs228/) gives ten weeks of topics with PGM chapter numbers, each homework's open and due dates, the grading split, a detailed late-day policy, and the topics of the five optional Friday TA sessions — d-separation, variable elimination, junction tree, Gibbs sampling, EM.
- **Notes**: [cs228-notes](https://ermongroup.github.io/cs228-notes/) is a full written treatment in its own right — preliminaries, representation, inference, learning, plus an extra chapter on variational autoencoders — with [source on GitHub](https://github.com/ermongroup/cs228-notes).
- **Videos**: closed; lecture recordings are on Canvas.
- **Assignments**: closed. The five homeworks go out on Ed; only the [LaTeX template](https://cs.stanford.edu/~ermon/cs228/hwtemplate.tex) is public.
- **Exams**: closed. A final was held March 21 but no paper is published.
- Further reading is listed and mostly free online: Wainwright and Jordan on variational inference, Mackay, Barber.

## Syllabus

[Ten weeks](https://ermongroup.github.io/cs228/), tracking the textbook.

1. Representation (weeks 1-2): probability theory, Bayesian networks, undirected models — PGM ch. 1-4.
2. Learning structure first (week 3): learning Bayes nets, ch. 16-17.
3. Inference (weeks 4-6): exact inference and message passing (ch. 9-10), sampling (ch. 12), MAP inference and structured prediction (ch. 13).
4. Learning (weeks 7-8): parameter learning (ch. 19-20), Bayesian and structure learning (ch. 17-18).
5. Theory (weeks 9-10): exponential families and variational inference (ch. 8, 11 and Wainwright-Jordan), then advanced topics.

## Prerequisites

- Probability and statistics, programming, algorithm design and analysis. The site offers a practical test: "If you are able to comfortably complete homework 1 then you likely have all the relevant background knowledge."
